# Act 60 and Tax Benefits

> **Research date:** November 22, 2026. Tax law in Puerto Rico changed twice in 2025–2026 (Act 65-2025, Act 38-2026), so treat every number here as something to confirm with a Puerto Rico tax attorney and CPA before acting.
> **Read this first.** This is the most important file in the set. Act 60 sets the firm's Puerto Rico tax rate, and the company's structure decides whether the founders also avoid U.S. federal income tax.

## TL;DR for agents

| Question | Short answer |
|---|---|
| What rate does an AI consulting firm pay? | **4%** Puerto Rico income tax on *export services* profit under an Act 60 **Chapter 3** decree. A **new** small or medium business (≤ $3M average gross income) pays **2% for its first 5 years**, then 4%. |
| What is the normal rate without a decree? | Graduated corporate tax up to **37.5%** (18.5% normal tax plus a surtax), with an alternative minimum tax of 18.5% (23% at ≥ $10M volume). |
| Are distributions to owners taxed? | Distributions out of export-services profit are **100% exempt** from Puerto Rico income tax. |
| Which clients qualify? | Only clients **outside Puerto Rico**, for work **with no Puerto Rico nexus**. Work for a Puerto Rico business, on Puerto Rico law, lobbying the Puerto Rico government, or about property used in Puerto Rico **does not qualify**. |
| Federal income tax? | For an owner who is a **bona fide resident** of Puerto Rico working **on the island** through a **Puerto Rico corporation**, generally **none** (IRC §933). Work done on the mainland is U.S.-source income and is taxed federally. |
| Biggest structural trap | Electing pass-through treatment for the LLC. Federal self-employment tax then applies, about **$31.6k per year on $300k** of profit. Keep the company taxed as a corporation. |
| Employees required? | None below **$3M** of annual business volume. Above that, one full-time Puerto Rico-resident employee, who can be a salaried owner. |
| Term | 15 years, extendable for 15 more. |
| Annual upkeep | DDEC annual report (**$500 fee + $5**), exempt-business return (Form 480.30(II)), separate books for decree and non-decree income. **No** $10k donation and **no** $5k fee: those belong to Chapter 2. |
| Urgent deadline | A founder who wants the **Chapter 2** individual-investor decree at **0%** must have a complete application **filed by December 31, 2026**, about 5 weeks from the research date. Applications filed from 2027 get **4%**. |

## 1. What Act 60 is

Act 60-2019, the Puerto Rico Incentives Code, consolidated the older incentive laws into one code. Many advisors still use the old names:

| Old name | Act 60 name | Covers |
|---|---|---|
| Act 20-2012 (Export Services Act) | **Chapter 3, Export Services** | The **business** |
| Act 22-2012 (Individual Investors Act) | **Chapter 2, Resident Individual Investor** | The **individual's** passive income: interest, dividends, capital gains |
| Act 135 (Young Entrepreneurs) | Young Entrepreneur incentive | New businesses started by founders under 36 |
| n/a | Difficult-to-hire employees, researchers and scientists, physicians | Individual employees |

Amendments to know:
- **Act 52-2022** changed compliance rules, fees and the donation structure.
- **Act 65-2025**: starting with tax year 2026, the Chapter 2 annual report moves from DDEC to Hacienda (the Treasury) as part of the income tax return.
- **Act 38-2026**, signed March 2026, extended the Chapter 2 investor program to **2055** and moved new applicants to a **4%** rate. It left the Chapter 3 export-services 4% rate unchanged.

Decrees are issued by **DDEC** (Departamento de Desarrollo Económico y Comercio) through its Incentives Portal. Processing commonly takes **60–120 days**.

## 2. Chapter 3: Export Services (the firm's decree)

### 2.1 Benefits

| Benefit | Standard decree | New SME (PyME) decree |
|---|---|---|
| Income tax on export-services net income | **4%** | **2% for 5 years**, then 4% |
| Distributions of decree earnings to shareholders | 100% exempt from PR income tax | 100% exempt |
| Personal and real property tax | 75% exempt | 100% exempt for 5 years, then 75% |
| Municipal license tax (*patente*) | 50% exempt | 100% exempt for 5 years, then 50% |
| Term | 15 + 15 years | 15 + 15 years |
| "Novel Pioneer Activity" | Rate can drop to **1%** (McConnell Valdés). Worth asking counsel whether a novel AI product qualifies. | |

**SME definition.** A *new* business whose gross income averages **$3M or less** over the prior three years, with no more than 500 employees. "New" matters: an existing firm that was already serving clients from Puerto Rico does not become new by applying.

**Base-period limitation.** If the business was already doing the eligible activity in Puerto Rico before it applied, the benefit applies only to income above a base period. **Apply before starting operations** (or at formation) so there is no base period.

### 2.2 Eligible services relevant to an AI firm

The DDEC and law-firm lists include: **Consulting**; **Development of computer programs**; **Research and development**; **Electronic data processing centers**; **Education services and training**; distribution via Internet or cloud, plus income from **licensing, subscriptions or service charges** (covers SaaS and AI products); shared service centers; marketing centers; and engineering and project management.

An AI consulting firm's core offerings (strategy, model and agent development, data engineering, MLOps, training, managed AI services, licensed IP) all fit these categories, provided the client is outside Puerto Rico.

### 2.3 The "no Puerto Rico nexus" rule (the most common mistake)

Service income does **not** qualify if the work:
1. relates to a business the client carries on **in Puerto Rico**,
2. advises on **Puerto Rico law or regulations**,
3. involves **lobbying the Puerto Rico government**, or
4. concerns property sold for use **in Puerto Rico**.

Consequences for the business model:
- **Local clients are allowed but taxed at regular rates.** That covers Puerto Rico SMEs, Puerto Rico banks, Puerto Rico government agencies, and pharmaceutical plants *located in Puerto Rico*. The firm keeps **separate books** for decree and non-decree income and expenses.
- A mainland pharmaceutical parent hiring the firm for work on its **Puerto Rico plant** is risky, because the work relates to a business carried on in Puerto Rico. Work for the parent's **global or mainland operations** fits the rule cleanly. Contracts and SOWs should state where the benefited operations are.
- Tag every client and SOW as *export* or *local* from day one (see §6).

### 2.4 Operational requirements

- A **real office or place of business in Puerto Rico**. A registered agent's address does not count. A home office can be enough for a one-person firm, depending on the work.
- **Employees:** none required below **$3M** of annual business volume, actual or projected. Above $3M, **one full-time Puerto Rico-resident employee**, who may be an owner working full time on salary. (One law-firm source phrases the threshold as "net income". The statute and most sources say *volume of business*. Confirm with counsel.)
- **No general salary requirement** for owners below $3M. Salary is still a planning decision: wages are taxed at regular Puerto Rico rates, not 4%, and carry FICA.
- **Annual report** to DDEC with a **$500 filing fee (+$5 transaction charge)**. Agreed-upon procedures or a compliance certification from a Puerto Rico-licensed CPA may be required. A missed annual report is the most common way decrees are lost, through fines of up to $10,000 and then revocation.
- **Exempt business income tax return:** Form 480.30(II).

### 2.5 Worked example (illustrative, from Hiltzik CPA, 2026)

Single owner, bona fide resident of Puerto Rico, $300,000 profit from export services, all work done on the island:

| | Taxed as a corporation (default) | Elected pass-through |
|---|---|---|
| Puerto Rico tax at 4% | $12,000 | $12,000 |
| Federal income tax | $0 | $0 |
| Social Security and Medicare | **$0** | **$31,605** |
| **Total** | **$12,000** | **$43,605** |

The table assumes the profit comes out as distributions, not salary. At the 2% new-SME rate the Puerto Rico tax would be **$6,000**. Without a decree the same profit could face Puerto Rico corporate tax of up to 37.5%.

## 3. Federal tax interaction (what Act 60 does *not* do)

Act 60 sets **Puerto Rico** tax only. The federal result depends on three things:

1. **Bona fide residency (IRC §937).** All three tests must be met each year:
   - **Presence test:** usually ≥ 183 days in Puerto Rico (other variants exist).
   - **Tax home test:** principal place of business is in Puerto Rico.
   - **Closer connection test:** closer ties to Puerto Rico than to the U.S. or any foreign country.
2. **Entity type.** A Puerto Rico corporation (or an LLC taxed as one, which is the default) is a *foreign* corporation for federal purposes. It owes no federal income tax on Puerto Rico-source income. Its dividends to a bona fide resident are excluded under **§933**, provided most of its income comes from an active business in Puerto Rico.
3. **Where the work is performed.** Income is sourced where the services are performed. Weeks spent working on site at a client's Chicago office produce **U.S.-source income**, even under a Puerto Rico decree. This matters a lot for consulting, which often needs on-site workshops. **Design delivery to be remote from Puerto Rico** and keep a travel log.

**Owners who do not live in Puerto Rico** still get the 4% Puerto Rico rate, but the U.S. generally taxes them each year on most of the company's income under the rules for U.S. owners of foreign corporations. For them the 4% is a Puerto Rico saving, not an overall one. This matters if some co-founders or investors stay on the mainland.

Bona fide residents still file federal returns for U.S.-source income and remain subject to **FBAR** and **FATCA (Form 8938)** reporting. Pre-move appreciation recognized within 10 years can still be taxed by the U.S. (Treas. Reg. §1.937-2(f)). Act 60 does not shield the federal estate tax.

## 4. Chapter 2: Resident Individual Investor (founders' personal decree)

This decree is separate from the firm's. It matters to founders with an investment portfolio.

| | Application **filed by Dec 31, 2026** | Application **filed Jan 1, 2027 or later** |
|---|---|---|
| Dividends and interest | **0%** until Dec 31, 2035 | **4%** until Dec 31, 2055 |
| Post-residency capital gains | **0%** until Dec 31, 2035 | **4%** until Dec 31, 2055 |
| Pre-residency gains (recognized ≥ 10 years after the move) | 5% until 2035 | 5% until 2055 |
| Look-back | Not a Puerto Rico resident between Jan 17, 2006 and Jan 17, 2012 | Not a Puerto Rico resident in the **6 years** before relocating |
| Home | Buy a principal residence in Puerto Rico within **2 years** | Same, and the title must be recorded in the Property Registry |

- **The filing date controls the rate**, not the move date or the approval date. Under DDEC Informative Bulletin 2026-004, the proof is the filing-fee payment timestamp on the Incentives Portal, by **11:59 p.m. on Dec 31, 2026**.
- Existing decree holders can apply to modify their decree into the 4%-to-2055 regime if a longer horizon suits them better.
- **Costs:** a **$5,000 application fee**; an **annual report fee of $5,000 + $5**; and an **annual donation of $10,000** from the second tax year. The donation splits into $5,000 to listed child-poverty nonprofits, $2,500 to another qualifying nonprofit and $2,500 to the Fondo Especial para la Igualdad Social. The individual must not control the recipients.
- The annual report was due **Nov 16** for tax year 2025, filed with DDEC. From tax year 2026 it is filed with Hacienda as part of the income tax return (Act 65-2025).

**Action item:** any founder considering relocation should decide **this month** whether to file a Chapter 2 application before Dec 31, 2026.

## 5. Other Act 60 incentives useful to the firm and its staff

Source: DDEC Act 60 brochure, which predates Act 38-2026. **Verify the current terms with DDEC.**

| Incentive | Eligibility | Benefit (as published by DDEC) |
|---|---|---|
| **Young Entrepreneur** | New business started by an individual **under 36** | 100% income tax exemption on the first **$500,000** of the new business's net income; 100% exemption on personal property and municipal taxes |
| **Difficult-to-hire employee** | Employee with specialized, indispensable knowledge working for an exempt business | 100% income tax exemption on wages and benefits **above $100,000** |
| **Researchers and scientists** | Research through a Higher Education Institution or an entity in the Science District | 100% income tax exemption on compensation up to $195,000 (university) or $250,000 (Science District) |
| **R&D credit** | Exempt business doing qualifying R&D in Puerto Rico | Tax credit of up to **50%** of R&D expenses |

Recruiting angle: the difficult-to-hire exemption can make senior AI hires from the mainland cheaper to bring over. See [06-staffing.md](06-staffing.md).

## 6. Compliance checklist for the firm (Chapter 3)

1. **Form the entity before applying**, and apply **before** serving clients from Puerto Rico, to avoid the base-period limitation.
2. **Keep the default corporate tax treatment.** Do not file Form SC 6045 or a federal check-the-box pass-through election without a priced analysis.
3. Open a **real Puerto Rico office** with a signed lease or coworking membership and keep proof.
4. Build a **client nexus register**: for each client and SOW, record whether it is *export* or *local* and why.
5. Keep **separate books** (or a separate entity) for decree and non-decree income.
6. Keep a **work-location log** for every owner and employee, because work done off the island is U.S.-source.
7. Track **business volume** against the **$3M** employee threshold and the SME definition.
8. Calendar the **DDEC annual report**, the Puerto Rico returns, municipal *patente* declarations, CRIM personal property returns and the CPA certification.
9. Founders: keep **bona fide residency** evidence (days log, home, driver's license, voter registration, banking, doctors, social ties).
10. Expect **IRS and Senate scrutiny** and document everything (see [08-risks.md](08-risks.md)).

## 7. Open questions for counsel

- Current DDEC **Chapter 3 application fee** and any **Act 52-2022** annual reporting changes for Chapter 3. Not confirmed in this research.
- Whether a mainland client's work touching its Puerto Rico plant can be carved into eligible and ineligible portions.
- Whether the "Novel Pioneer Activity" 1% rate is realistic for an AI product line.
- Whether to run separate entities (export firm vs. local firm) or one entity with segregated books.
- How the IVU sales tax applies to export services billed to non-Puerto Rico clients (see [05-permits-and-licenses.md](05-permits-and-licenses.md)).
- Whether the Young Entrepreneur incentive is still available and can be combined with Chapter 3.
